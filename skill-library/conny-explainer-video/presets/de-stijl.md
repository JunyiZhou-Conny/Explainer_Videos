## Visual grammar

- Make **relationships between planes** the organizing idea. Unequal rectangles, strong horizontal/vertical boundaries, and deliberate uncolored space carry the composition. A thick-line grid is part of the visible subject, rather than merely a hidden alignment aid.
- Use a warm light ground with a restricted primary-color vocabulary and near-black rules. Leave enough light area for explanations; do not fill every region or give every region equal visual weight.
- Balance is achieved by the positions and areas of the colored planes and their relationship to empty space. The reference builds lines, introduces color, then changes the geometry to discuss balance around 00:45–01:40. It explicitly contrasts asymmetry with bilateral symmetry.
- Use direct bold sans-serif labels and small numbered chapter markers. Text stays legible and follows the geometry; letters do not become a decorative puzzle.
- Carry the same spatial language across examples. The episode translates its subject from flat composition into a chair and a house around 02:15–02:40. For a software topic, reuse the same colored component as the explanation moves from a file, to a package, to a workflow.

The source distinguishes a broader movement from one painter, and later separates it from Bauhaus, Swiss design, Memphis, and minimalism. Preserve that distinction. Do not label every red/yellow/blue shape arrangement “Mondrian” or every grid “De Stijl.” The reference also discusses a diagonal variation in its historical comparison; the canonical orthogonal preset below is a deliberate production choice, not a claim that every work in the movement followed one rule.

## Motion and practical recipes

The detailed frames show a tiny square expanding into a long horizontal boundary, one composition replacing another while the chapter index advances, and the final summary receding into a larger composition of earlier scenes. Translate those behaviors into original sequences:

1. **Partition a problem.** Begin with an empty plane and one named object. Extend a horizontal boundary, then a vertical branch. Add labels only after the regions are visually established. Reveal color after the structure, so viewers understand what it encodes.
2. **Make room for an idea.** Move one divider and resize its neighboring regions together over about 0.8–1.2 seconds. Preserve the objects attached to each region. Use this to show separating a reusable workflow from episode-specific content.
3. **Same source, several uses.** Hold a small canonical skill plane at one anchor. Extend three orthogonal routes to agent regions. Present references as connections, keeping the source visually singular. Color indicates identity, not a claim that disk copies are made.
4. **Compare a construction rule.** Show the same content in two distinct partitions, then point to the changed boundary or space allocation. A brief guide line may explain symmetry or alignment and then disappear. Let viewers see the reason for the layout change.
5. **Recap as a whole.** Pull back from the active scene into a larger, newly composed map containing a few earlier diagrams. The map should reveal the lesson's relationships, not reproduce the source video's exact page or poster.

Use restrained linear or smooth cubic movement, with no rubbery bounce. Typical line extensions can take 0.4–0.7 seconds, followed by reading holds; these timings are original starting points and should follow the narration. Axis-aligned wipes and shared-boundary transformations suit the preset better than repeated unrelated fades.

## Avoid and accept

Avoid using the primary colors as a sticker palette on rounded dashboard cards. Avoid gradients, heavy shadows, arbitrary diagonals in the canonical layout, decorative circles/triangles as the main compositional system, and copying a famous painting's exact rectangle arrangement. Illustrative objects can retain the shape needed to explain them; the reference itself uses exceptions when teaching comparisons and examples.

Acceptance: the viewer can see which boundary or relationship each animation explains; uncolored space does useful work; the composition remains balanced without becoming mechanically symmetric; labels and captions are readable; an episode is visibly more than generic slides recolored red/yellow/blue.
